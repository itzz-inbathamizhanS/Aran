## intro | Welcome | Aran: a firewall for data
This is Aran, explained from zero.
Aran is a Tamil word. It means fortress, a protective wall.
It was built by four students at Kalasalingam Academy of Research and Education.
Inbathamizhan S leads the project, with Aasif Ahmed A on cybersecurity, and SN Arun Vijay and Bharadhwaj M on AI and machine learning.
Every technical word you hear will be shown as a picture, so you do not need any background to follow along.
We will go in order: the problem, the research, how Aran works, how it was built, the results, and what comes next.

## problem | Chapter 1 | The problem
Every day a bank handles huge amounts of customer data.
That data travels through many paths: API calls between apps, log files, exports to other companies, support tools, and now AI chatbots.
The problem statement asked for one thing: find sensitive customer data in API traffic and logs, and protect it before it is stored or shared.
Leaks are not imaginary. In 2016 about 3.2 million Indian debit cards were exposed through malware at a payment processor.
In 2020, 35 million records of masked card data from Juspay were found for sale on the dark web.
In 2024, Star Health customer records were sold through Telegram bots.
Even Meta was fined 91 million euros for writing passwords into its logs in plain text.
In India, the DPDP Act can fine a company up to 250 crore rupees for failing to protect personal data.

## identifiers | Chapter 2 | What we protect
First, a word you will hear often: PII, personally identifiable information. It is any data that points to one real person.
Aadhaar is India's 12 digit national ID. Its last digit is special. It is a check digit, and we will see why that matters.
PAN is the income tax number. Ten characters: five letters, four digits, and one letter.
A payment card number is usually 16 digits. The first six or eight digits name the bank that issued it, and the last digit is also a check digit.
A UPI ID looks like an email address, such as ravi@oksbi. || A U P I I D looks like an email address, such as rah-vee, at, okay S B I.
An OTP is a one time password: just a few random digits, with no pattern at all. That makes it the hardest thing to find.
Mobile numbers, names and addresses complete the list.
Every example number in this video is generated. None of them belongs to a real person, because using a real one would itself be a leak.

## words | Chapter 3 | The basic words
Data moves from a source, like the mobile app, to a sink. A sink is any place data ends up: a log platform, a CRM, a vendor, or an AI provider.
That movement is called a data flow.
A choke point is a place every flow must pass through, like a gate in a wall.
A firewall normally blocks connections. Aran is a firewall for data. It lets the traffic through, but changes the sensitive values inside it.
Masking hides part of a value, like showing only the last four digits. It cannot be undone.
Tokenisation swaps the real value for a stand in called a token. The token has the same shape, so the bank's software keeps working.
Only a system with permission can swap the token back. That is called detokenising, and Aran records every time it happens.

## regex | Chapter 4 | Why simple search fails
The obvious solution is a pattern search, called a regular expression, or regex. For example: find any run of six digits and call it an OTP.
When a detector raises an alarm on something harmless, that is a false positive, or false alarm.
When it misses something real, that is a false negative.
To measure this honestly, the team used Loghub: 32,000 real lines of logs from 16 real systems.
These logs contain no Indian identifiers at all. So every single hit is a false alarm.
A plain OTP pattern fired 67,481 times on those 32,000 lines.
The word OTP even matched inside the word hotplug.
Aran's checks, which we will meet next, raised just 6.
Too many false alarms make teams switch the detector off, and then the real leaks go through.

## research | Chapter 5 | How the research was done
The research followed six steps.
First, reframe the problem. The real problem is not finding PII with regex. A bank cannot prove where each customer's identifiers are, who can read them, and which copy leaked.
Second, write the obvious solution first, the baseline, so there is something concrete to beat.
Third, a literature survey: the laws a bank must follow, real leaks, and research papers, about 40 sources in all.
Fourth, measure the gaps on real data instead of just arguing about them.
Fifth, for each gap, find a piece of mathematics that closes it, and prove what can be proven.
Sixth, attack the design. Eleven weaknesses were found, and every fix became part of the design.
The measurement found six research gaps.
One: number detectors drown in false alarms. Two: a protected number and a leaked number look exactly the same.
Three: nobody measures how much a mask still reveals. Four: when a leak appears outside, nobody can say which system leaked it.
Five: erasure cannot reach logs, backups and vendors. And six: OTPs have no structure at all.
Each gap became one of Aran's mechanisms. Let us see them, starting with the most important idea.

## checkdigit | Chapter 6 | Check digits
A check digit is one extra digit calculated from all the other digits.
If you type one digit wrong, the calculation no longer matches, so the number is invalid. Testing this is called a checksum.
Card numbers use the Luhn method, invented at IBM in 1960. Let us run it.
Take the body 7992739871. Starting from the right, double every second digit. || Take the body 7, 9, 9, 2, 7, 3, 9, 8, 7, 1. Starting from the right, double every second digit.
Any result above nine has nine taken away. So 16 becomes 7, and 18 becomes 9.
Add everything up. The total is 67.
The check digit is what brings the total to the next multiple of ten. That is 3.
Aadhaar uses a stronger method, the Verhoeff algorithm, from 1969.
It is built on the ten ways you can rotate or flip a pentagon. Mathematicians call this the dihedral group.
In this group, the order of moves matters. Rotate then flip is not the same as flip then rotate. That is exactly why Verhoeff catches two swapped digits.
So Verhoeff catches every single wrong digit and every swap of two neighbours. A real Aadhaar always passes.

## tokens | Chapter 7 | Mechanism 1: tokens that always fail
Here is Aran's key idea. Everyone uses check digits only to catch typing mistakes. Aran turns them around.
Real numbers always pass the checksum. So Aran builds every token to always fail it.
First, encryption. Encryption scrambles data with a secret number called a key. Only someone with the key can unscramble it.
Aran uses AES, the world's standard cipher, inside a method called FF1, approved by NIST, the US standards body.
FF1 is format preserving. Twelve digits go in, twelve digits come out.
Inside, FF1 is a Feistel network. It splits the number into two halves, scrambles one half using the other and the key, swaps them, and repeats ten times. It can always be run backwards.
Aran encrypts the first 11 digits, then calculates the correct check digit for the new body.
Then it adds a secret offset between one and nine, so the last digit is always wrong.
This gives Theorem 1. Every real Aadhaar passes Verhoeff, and no Aran token ever does.
So anyone, anywhere, can tell protected data from leaked data, with zero ambiguity.
In the test, 20,000 tokens were placed in real logs, and not one was flagged as real. All 20,000 real values were caught.

## persink | Chapter 8 | Mechanism 3: tokens that name the leaker
FF1 takes one more input, called a tweak. It changes the output without changing the key.
Aran uses the sink's name as the tweak. So the same customer gets a different token in every destination.
Here is one generated Aadhaar, and its three tokens: one for the CRM, one for the loan vendor, and one for the log platform.
Think of marked banknotes. Each vendor gets differently marked copies.
Now suppose a file with a token appears on Telegram.
Anyone can check it fails Verhoeff, so it is a token, not a raw Aadhaar.
And Aran's trace says this token was only ever issued to the loan vendor. The leaked file names its own source.
Theorem 2 puts a number on it. For a bank with 50 million customers and 40 sinks, a dump of five tokens is wrongly traced at most 3 times in a billion.
In the test, dumps were traced to the right sink 100 percent of the time.

## detect | Chapter 9 | Mechanism 2: detection as evidence
Detection is treated as a decision built from evidence, not a pattern match.
Each clue has a likelihood ratio: how much more likely it is if the value is real than if it is noise.
For example, passing the checksum is ten times more likely for a real number than for random digits.
Bayes' rule multiplies the clues together, turning a starting guess into a probability.
If that probability crosses a threshold, the value is treated as sensitive. Each sink can have its own threshold.
The detector is also calibrated. When it says ninety percent sure, it is right ninety percent of the time.
The strongest clues come from what the bank already owns.
A hash is a one way fingerprint. The same input always gives the same fingerprint, but you cannot go backwards.
An HMAC is a hash that needs a secret key, so only the bank can make the fingerprints.
Aran keeps the fingerprints of its own customers' numbers in a cuckoo filter, a compact table that answers: have I seen this before? Unlike older designs, it can also delete entries.
For OTPs, Aran remembers each OTP it issued, bound to the exact request it belongs to.
Without that binding, a random six digit number would match some live OTP 7 percent of the time. With it, about one in a million.
On 16,000 real log lines, false alarms fell from 2,506 with regex to just 60.

## ai | Chapter 10 | The AI layer
Some data has no checksum at all, like names and addresses. That is where AI comes in.
Machine learning means a program learns patterns from examples, instead of being given rules.
Training data teaches the model. Test data, kept separate, checks it on things it has never seen.
NER, named entity recognition, finds names and places in text.
Aran uses a CRF, a fast model that labels each word while looking at its neighbours. It runs on a normal laptop.
It works in English, in Hindi, and in Hinglish, where both languages mix in one sentence.
For OTPs and UPI IDs, a second classifier reads slices of characters around the number, to tell an OTP from a port number, or a UPI ID from an email.
Scores are measured with precision, how much of what was flagged was real, and recall, how much of what was real got flagged.
AI is used exactly where maths cannot give an exact answer, and nowhere else. This part is owned by the AI and machine learning pair.

## normaliser | Chapter 11 | Disguises and typos
Attackers and careless code can hide a number. Tamil or Hindi digits, dots between digits, spaces, invisible characters, web escapes, or base64 encoding.
The normaliser undoes 12 kinds of disguise before detection, and remembers where each character came from.
Hidden identifiers caught went from 8.3 percent to 100 percent.
What about a real Aadhaar with one typo? It now fails Verhoeff, just like a token.
Typo repair tries all 108 single digit edits and all 11 swaps of neighbours, and checks each one against the bank's own customer list.
One typo identifiers caught went from zero to 99.2 percent, with no tokens mistaken for typos.
A red team then attacked Aran with 26 hiding tricks. 22 of them are now fully caught.

## ledger | Chapter 12 | Mechanism 4: the leakage ledger
Masks look safe, but they leak. Information is measured in bits. Each bit is one yes or no answer.
If one thousand possible numbers remain, that is about 10 bits. A computer can try them all in seconds.
Min-entropy measures exactly this: how hard the best single guess is.
Showing the first eight and the last four digits of a card leaves only 1,000 candidates.
The bigger danger is joining. Send the first six digits to one system, and the last four to another system of the same vendor.
Together they leave only 100,000 candidates.
Aran's ledger remembers what each group of connected systems has already seen, called a trust domain.
It refuses any disclosure that would leave a customer below 20 bits, about one million candidates.
In the test, 14,175 customers could be narrowed down by joining. The ledger brought that to zero.

## erasure | Chapter 13 | Mechanism 5: erasure with proof
The law gives people the right to have their data erased. But copies live in logs, backups and vendor systems.
Aran keeps a vault: a protected table linking each random token to its real value, locked with a key.
There is one key per customer, per purpose: servicing, marketing, loans, and KYC.
Crypto-shredding means destroying the key. Every copy of that customer's tokens, everywhere, becomes meaningless at once.
Some data must be kept by law. KYC records have a legal hold. So the KYC key is time locked, and Aran refuses to erase it early, then destroys it automatically when retention ends.
Theorem 3 says that once the key is destroyed, the tokens reveal nothing about the customer.
Every erasure is written into a Merkle tree. Each record becomes a hash, and each pair of hashes is hashed again, up to a single root.
Change any record and the root changes, so the log cannot be secretly edited.
The customer gets a receipt with an inclusion proof: the few hashes needed to rebuild the root.
The receipt is also digitally signed with a private key, so anyone with the public key can check it was not forged.
Even the customer's phone can check the receipt by itself.

## immune | Chapter 14 | The immune layer
Even a perfect detector cannot see an insider who detokenises thousands of records at 2 a.m. For this, Aran borrows from biology.
In your body, the thymus trains immune cells. Any cell that attacks your own body is destroyed. Only cells that ignore self survive.
This is called negative selection.
Aran describes each data flow with seven numbers: volume, hour, how much raw data, how often it is detokenised, and so on. Each flow is a point in space.
Normal flows form the self region.
Aran scatters random detectors, deletes every one that touches self, and keeps the rest.
Now the insider's 2 a.m. flow lands inside a detector, and an alert fires.
On simulated attacks, the immune layer caught all six attack types, with only 0.6 percent false alarms. A standard method, Isolation Forest, caught 20 percent.
And like real immunity, it remembers. A repeat attack scored 0.71 instead of 0.37.
This layer was the team lead's biology plan, owned by the cybersecurity pair.

## gateway | Chapter 15 | The AI gateway
Bank staff now paste customer details into AI chatbots, called large language models, or LLMs.
The text you send to one is called a prompt.
The AI gateway sits between staff and the AI provider.
Each identifier becomes a typed placeholder, like Aadhaar one, before the provider ever sees it.
The model answers using the placeholders. Then real values are filled back only for staff whose role allows it.
Every fill back is written to a hash chain, where each entry contains the hash of the one before. Editing any old entry breaks the chain.
Prompt injection tries to trick the model into revealing data. Five such attacks were tested, and none leaked a value.
Raw values reaching the provider fell from 100 percent to 7.8 percent, and every type of number went to zero.

## quantum | Chapter 16 | Post-quantum security
A future quantum computer could break today's public key cryptography.
Shor's algorithm breaks the key exchange and signatures used across the internet today.
Grover's algorithm only halves the strength of keys like AES, so Aran moved to AES-256.
Attackers can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it later. This is called harvest now, decrypt later.
That led to a new finding. If a customer's key was ever backed up using classical key exchange, a recording of that traffic could bring the key back after erasure.
In the simulation, 100 percent of erased values came back from the classical backups.
The fix is hybrid cryptography: a classical method and a new post quantum method together, so the result is safe if either one holds.
Aran pairs X25519 with ML-KEM, and Ed25519 with ML-DSA, the new NIST standards from 2024. || Aran pairs X twenty-five five nineteen with M L kem, and Ed twenty-five five nineteen with M L D S A, the new NIST standards from 2024.
With the hybrid, zero percent of erased values came back.
Aran also produces a CBOM, a list of every cryptographic algorithm it uses, as India's banking roadmap asks for.

## walkthrough | Chapter 17 | One request, start to finish
Let us follow one request. Ravi, a made up customer, applies for a loan in the mobile app.
The message holds Ravi's name, Aadhaar, PAN, mobile, address and an OTP. It is going to a loan vendor.
Step one, normalise: undo any disguises.
Step two, detect: checksums, AI models and registries find every sensitive value, each with a calibrated probability.
Step three, decide: the leakage ledger checks what this vendor has already seen.
Step four, protect. The Aadhaar becomes a token issued only to this vendor. PAN, mobile, name and address become keyed labels. The OTP is destroyed.
Step five, record: the flow goes to the immune layer.
The vendor still gets data in exactly the right shape, so its systems keep working.
If the vendor leaks, the token names it. If Ravi asks for erasure, Ravi's keys are destroyed and a signed receipt follows. If an insider misbehaves, the immune layer raises an alert.

## results | Chapter 19 | Results
Every result comes from an experiment script, so anyone can run it again.
On 16,000 real log lines from 8 systems the models never saw, plain regex removed 46.1 percent of sensitive values.
The AI layers reached 83.5 percent. The normaliser and registries took it to 89.7. The mobile and OTP fixes reached 91 percent, without any registries.
Aadhaar, card, PAN and UPI have been at 100 percent since the first AI run.
OTP false alarms fell from 67,481 to 6. Tokens wrongly flagged as real: zero out of 20,000. Leaked dumps traced correctly: 100 percent.
Customers exposed by joining systems: 14,175 down to zero. Raw values reaching an AI provider: 100 percent down to 7.8.

## limits | Chapter 20 | Honest limits and next steps
Good research states its weak spots plainly.
Names are the weakest type, at about 77 percent. A transformer model, the technology behind modern AI, is the planned fix.
Two new mistakes appeared after the mobile and OTP fix, and must be repaired.
Speed is 654 lines per second in Python. A bank needs thousands per core, so a faster port is planned.
The flow attacks were simulated, the AI gateway has not been tested with a real model yet, and erasure by key destruction needs a lawyer's view.
Next, the cybersecurity pair will make the vault survive restarts, build the raw dump tracer, and add post quantum encryption in front of the server.
The AI and machine learning pair will fix the two mistakes, add the transformer model for names, and test the gateway with a real model.
Later come Tamil name detection, reading ID card photos, and a research paper.

## close | Summary | Aran in thirty seconds
Banks leak customer data through logs, vendors and AI chatbots, not just databases.
Aran is a firewall for data that sits on those paths, and swaps every identifier for a look alike token before anything is stored or shared.
Its tokens always fail the check digit, so anyone can prove whether a number is protected or leaked.
Each destination gets different tokens, so a leaked file names its source.
And each customer's data is locked under its own key, so erasing a customer is one key deletion, with a signed receipt.
Aran: protection you can prove. Thank you for watching.
